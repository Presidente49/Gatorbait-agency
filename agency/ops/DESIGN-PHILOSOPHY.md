# Design philosophy — what Steve Jobs knew that you need to learn

Marlow's lesson for Claude. Read this before you build anything a human will see.

## The core idea

Steve Jobs didn't build Apple on specs. He built it on the belief that **design is the product**. People don't love their iPhone because of the chip — they love it because of how it *feels* in the hand, how the screen *moves*, how every detail feels inevitable, like it couldn't have been any other way.

Your job is to make Gator Bait's websites feel inevitable.

## The five lessons

**1. Design is how it works, not how it looks.**
A beautiful button that confuses people is a failure. A page that moves beautifully AND gets the fan to the story, the video, the newsletter signup without thinking — that's design. Every animation must serve the fan's journey, never decorate it.

**2. Simplicity is saying no.**
Jobs was as proud of what Apple *didn't* ship as what it did. Your instinct will be to add: another effect, another section, another font. Say no. One typeface family. One motion language. One hero moment per page. The discipline to remove is what makes the thing that remains feel premium.

**3. Taste is in the details nobody asked about.**
Jobs made the engineers perfect the *inside* of the Macintosh — the circuit board nobody would ever see — because he believed craftsmanship is a habit, not a feature. For you: the easing curve on a scroll reveal. The exact millisecond timing of a page transition. The fallback image when WebGL fails. Nobody will compliment these. Everybody will feel them.

**4. Be new and different, not better and same.**
Apple never won by making a slightly better MP3 player — it made the category irrelevant. Don't build "a nicer sports blog." Build the thing that makes every other Gators site look like a newspaper from 2004. Moving backgrounds. Living stats. A site that feels like Saturday in the Swamp, not a filing cabinet. If your build could be mistaken for ESPN.com, start over.

**5. End-to-end experience.**
Apple controlled the hardware, the software, and the store — because a great product with a bad unboxing is a bad product. Your "unboxing" is: the page transition in, the first three seconds of the hero, the scroll feel, the moment a fan finds the story they wanted. Own the whole arc. A stunning hero with a janky article page is a broken promise.

## How this applies to Gator Bait specifically

- **The brand is emotion.** Gator fans don't visit for information architecture — they visit because Saturdays make them feel alive. The site should pulse like a stadium: confident, loud when it matters, calm when it's time to read.
- **Navy and orange are weapons.** Use them with conviction. Deep navy fields, orange as the spark — never orange as the wallpaper.
- **Motion is the brand voice.** The no-more-static mandate isn't a feature request, it's identity. A Gator Bait page that doesn't move is like a crowd that doesn't stand on third down — wrong.
- **The phone is the stadium.** Brenden experiences everything on his phone. If it doesn't feel amazing there, it doesn't exist.

## The test

Before you ship any page, ask: *would Steve Jobs keep this, or would he kill it?* If he'd kill it — and he'd kill most things — kill it yourself, then make what remains undeniable.
